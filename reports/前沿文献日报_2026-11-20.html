1AlphaInterp: Revealing how AlphaFold 3 processes structure, confidence, and evolutionary information
PLoS Computational Biology · IF 3.6 · 2026-11-19
📄 How does evolutionary information become structural knowledge? AlphaFold 3 predicts protein structures and biomolecular assemblies with remarkable accuracy, yet how it transforms comparative sequence information into three-dimensional structure remains poorly understood. Here, we use mechanistic interpretability to follow this transformation through AlphaFold 3 and identify a computational principle underlying its structural inference. By extracting single- and pair-representations across the model’s forward pass and combining representational-geometry analysis, linear probing, activation patching, and perturbation of the multiple-sequence alignment (MSA) and its phylogenetic diversity, we show that AlphaFold 3 does not simply infer structure from raw sequence nor from MSA depth. Instead, a small number of sufficiently divergent homologues provides enough comparative information to largely recover the structure predicted using the full MSA. The pairformer transforms this evolutionary signal into a compact latent space in which residue-pair distances, contacts, fold similarity, and structural confidence become progressively decodable. Removing the MSA disrupts these latent geometries. Replacing it with evolutionarily similar homologues provides substantially less information, while evolutionarily unrelated sequences fail to rescue prediction despite preserving the formal alignment input. In contrast, phylogenetically diverse MSA subsampling largely restores both prediction accuracy and representational coherence. These results suggest that AlphaFold 3 has learned to infer structure by constraining its geometric priors and structural hypotheses by the sequence variation of the MSA. This framework helps explain both the model’s remarkable predictive power and its characteristic failure modes, with direct implications for protein design, conformational diversity, out-of-distribution generalization, and the interpretation of model confidence.

📄 Background: Large language models (LLMs) are increasingly explored for clinical applications in neurology, yet their real-world utility, safety, and optimal implementation remain uncertain. We systematically reviewed the literature to characterize current applications, evaluate evidence quality, and identify knowledge gaps regarding LLM use in clinical neurology. Methods: Following PRISMA guidelines, we searched PubMed, Embase, Scopus, Web of Science, and CENTRAL from January 1, 2022 through February 1 2026. for peer-reviewed studies evaluating LLM applications in clinical neurology. We included studies using large language models for clinically relevant neurology tasks from text or multimodal inputs. Two independent reviewers screened records, extracted data, and assessed risk of bias using the QUADS-AI. We synthesized evidence narratively across application domains, validation approaches, and model performance. Results: Thirty-six studies (published 2023-2026) spanning 8 neurology subspecialties met inclusion criteria; 13 were simulation or feasibility studies, 17 analyzed retrospective clinical data, and 6 reported prospective clinical validation. Proprietary models predominated; 7 studies used retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) and 3 used agentic frameworks. Performance was highest for constrained tasks, including binary diagnostic classification (area under the curve, AUC 0.75-0.94), information extraction (F1 score, 0.89-0.90), patient education question answering (accuracy, 68%-97%), and ischemic stroke thrombectomy decision support (AUC, 0.92). Conclusion: LLMs show promise for selected neurology workflows, but current evidence is early, heterogeneous, and limited by high risk of bias and scarce prospective validation. Clinical translation will likely require RAG and agentic architectures that can plan multi-step tasks, retrieve guidelines and local protocols, verify and calibrate outputs, and produce structured, auditable recommendations with source attribution, with clinician oversight and prospective evaluation.
